# Hypotheses: Why Calibration Is Failing

## The Situation

We faithfully implemented the Birchfield et al. (2017) algorithm. We ran it on **Texas-7k bus positions** and compared the generated topology against the **Texas-7k reference topology**. Aggregate metrics (m/n, mean degree, meshedness) match well at 345 kV. But the topology has the wrong **shape**: too many degree-1 stubs, lines too short, and the problem is dramatically worse at 138 kV (deg1_frac = 22.5% vs target 7.7%).

Six experiments varying scoring weights, quotas, distance penalties, and connectivity decay produced only a 43% error reduction at 345 kV, and the best config doesn't generalize to 138 kV.

---

## Hypothesis 1: We Are Comparing Against the Wrong Target

**The Texas-7k is not a Birchfield-generated grid.** It is a hand-crafted synthetic case built by Texas A&M researchers to represent the real ERCOT grid's behavior. It was designed for power systems simulation fidelity, not to satisfy Birchfield's structural statistics.

The Birchfield paper is explicit about what it produces: *"The synthetic network has no relation to the actual grid in Texas, except that the load and generation profiles are similar."* Their validation criteria are aggregate statistics averaged over **28 Eastern and 15 Western utility networks** (Table III). They never claim to reproduce any specific grid's degree distribution or line length histogram.

The Texas-7k has m/n = 1.55 at 345 kV, but Birchfield targets m/n = 1.22. The Texas-7k has 10.45% intersection rate; Birchfield targets < 20% (Table VI shows 6.5% for their own 345 kV output). We forced our generator to target m/n = 1.55 to match Texas-7k — but this means we're asking the Birchfield algorithm to produce 56% more edges than it was designed for, which fundamentally changes the selection dynamics.

**Implication:** The "correct" output of the Birchfield algorithm on these bus positions would have m/n ≈ 1.22, ~316 edges at 345 kV, and would look tree-like — because that IS what m/n = 1.22 produces. The degree distribution mismatch may be inherent to the algorithm at any parameterization, not a bug.

**Test:** Run the algorithm at its native m/n = 1.22 and compare structural statistics against Birchfield's own 2000-bus case (Table VI), not against Texas-7k. If the statistics match Birchfield's published results, the algorithm is correct and the comparison target is wrong.

## Hypothesis 2: The Connectivity Bonus Creates Degree-1 Nodes By Design

The paper's Table V specifies: *"-300 if segment contributes to connectivity at single voltage level, **or connects to a radial substation**"*. Our implementation gives +300 (= `w_conn_v`) when either endpoint has degree 0.

In early iterations, this is correct: it connects isolated nodes. But the mechanism is:
1. Node A has degree 0 → candidate (A, B) gets +300 bonus → A gets its first edge → degree 1
2. Now A has degree 1. No more bonus. The algorithm moves on to connect the next degree-0 node.
3. Node A stays at degree 1 forever unless some other scoring term happens to favor adding a second edge to it.

The result: a wave of degree-1 nodes created in sequence. Each node gets exactly one edge before attention moves to the next unconnected node. This is the **spur factory**.

At 345 kV (259 nodes, 405 edges), enough edges exist that most nodes eventually get a second edge by accident. At 138 kV (3270 nodes, 4186 edges), the ratio is tighter (m/n = 1.28 vs 1.55), and K=32 per iteration means the algorithm sprints through connecting nodes without backfilling.

**The paper's phrase "connects to a radial substation" is ambiguous.** Does it mean:
- (a) A node with degree 0 (our implementation), or
- (b) A node with degree 1 (a radial/spur node that needs a second line for reliability)?

If interpretation (b), the bonus would persist until degree 2, which would naturally suppress degree-1 nodes. This is a small but critical difference.

**Test:** Change the degree-0 check to degree ≤ 1 and re-run. If deg1_frac drops dramatically, this is the dominant cause.

## Hypothesis 3: The Paper's Algorithm Was Never Meant to Run on Pre-existing Bus Positions

The Birchfield paper synthesizes substations AND topology together. Their substation placement (Section II) uses hierarchical clustering of postal codes, producing substations that are roughly evenly spaced with population-weighted density. The topology algorithm was tuned for those spatial distributions.

Texas-7k bus positions were NOT generated by the Birchfield substation algorithm. They represent a different spatial distribution — one that mimics a real grid, with its own clustering patterns, urban/rural density ratios, and generation-load separation distances.

The Delaunay triangulation of Texas-7k buses produces a different candidate set than the triangulation of Birchfield-synthesized substations would. Candidate edge lengths, neighbor distances, and spatial connectivity patterns are different. The scoring weights in Table V (w_dist = +2/mile, w_dc = -0.5×Pest, etc.) were empirically tuned for Birchfield's own substation distributions, not for arbitrary bus positions.

**Implication:** The scoring weights need re-tuning for each spatial distribution. There is no universal parameterization.

**Test:** Generate substations using Dartboard's own Stage 1-2 pipeline for Texas, then run topology generation on THOSE positions. Compare structural statistics to both Birchfield's published results and to Texas-7k. If Dartboard-substations produce better structural statistics than Texas-7k-buses, the spatial distribution is the issue.

## Hypothesis 4: The "Overall Connectivity" Bonus Is Misimplemented

The paper's Table V says: *"-1000 if segment contributes to full system connectivity under single node removal"*. This is the N-1 connectivity criterion — the combined multi-voltage graph should remain connected even if one substation is removed.

Our implementation (`_compute_score_cheap`, line 613-616):
```python
if self._find(idx_i) != self._find(idx_j):
    score += self.config.w_conn_v      # +300
    score += self.config.w_conn_overall # +1000
```

This applies `w_conn_overall` whenever an edge **merges two disconnected components** — the same condition as `w_conn_v`. But the paper distinguishes them:
- `w_conn_v = 300`: single-voltage-level connectivity
- `w_conn_overall = 1000`: **full system connectivity under single node removal** (articulation point elimination)

These are fundamentally different conditions. Merging components is about basic connectivity. The N-1 criterion is about **biconnectivity** — eliminating articulation points. An edge that doesn't merge components (both endpoints already connected) can still earn the +1000 bonus if it makes the graph biconnected by providing a redundant path around an articulation point.

Our implementation never rewards edges that create redundant paths (cycles). It only rewards edges that merge disconnected components. This means:
- **Cycle-forming edges get NO connectivity bonus at all** once the graph is connected
- The only incentive for cycles is the category quota system (which allows Delaunay and neighbor edges)
- The massive +1000 bonus that should incentivize mesh-forming edges is instead wasted on the same component-merging edges that already get +300

This could be the single largest cause of the tree-like topology. The algorithm has a 1000-point incentive for biconnectivity that is being applied to the wrong condition.

**Test:** Implement proper articulation point detection (Tarjan's algorithm, O(V+E)) and apply `w_conn_overall` only when an edge eliminates an articulation point. This should strongly incentivize cycle-forming edges that create mesh structure.

## Hypothesis 5: Two-Phase Scoring Distorts Line Length Distribution

The audit report (Part 2, "CRITICAL FINDING") identified this: the iteration loop scores ALL candidates with `_compute_score_cheap()` first, then only the top K×5 get intersection checking. Before commit cb292d6, DC flow was also restricted to the top K×5; it's now applied to all candidates.

But the distance penalty dominates cheap scoring. A 100 km line gets -124 from distance; a 10 km line gets -12. Once connectivity bonuses are exhausted (graph is connected, no degree-0 nodes), the ranking is almost purely by line length — shortest first.

This means:
1. Short lines fill all K slots every iteration
2. Long lines are perpetually outcompeted
3. The only long lines added are MST edges (which get connectivity bonus) and early-iteration edges

The paper adds lines *"at each of 420 iterations"* with K=5. For their 225-node 345 kV network, that's 287 edges over 420 iterations, or ~0.68 edges per iteration. Our algorithm at 345 kV adds K=5 per iteration over ~80 iterations. At 138 kV, K=32 per iteration.

**The batch size matters.** With K=5, the 5 shortest competitive lines are added per round, then scores are recomputed. With K=32, the 32 shortest lines are grabbed simultaneously — the same short-biased selection is amplified 6×.

**Test:** This is partially what Experiment 5 tested (K=1 did show different behavior — more precise m/n at 1.552 exactly). A deeper test: set K=1 and run at 138 kV. If the degree distribution improves significantly, batch size is a factor.

## Hypothesis 6: The Category Quota System Is Interpreted Backwards

The paper's Table III shows:

| Category | Eastern Interconnect |
|---|---|
| Minimum Spanning Tree | 47.8% |
| **Delaunay Triangulation** | **75.6%** |
| Delaunay 2 neighbor | 18.3% |
| Delaunay 3 neighbor | 4.6% |

The "Delaunay Triangulation" line at 75.6% is **cumulative** — it means 75.6% of real lines fall on either the MST or the Delaunay triangulation. The individual Delaunay-only fraction is 75.6% - 47.8% = 27.8%.

But Table VI of the 2000-bus case shows:

| Category | Target Quota | 345 kV result |
|---|---|---|
| MST | 50% | 49.1% |
| Delaunay | 70% | 69.7% |
| 2-neighbor | 25% | 24.7% |
| 3-neighbor | 5% | 5.6% |

These quotas sum to 150%, which confirms they are **cumulative** (MST ⊂ Delaunay). The "Delaunay 70%" means 70% of edges fall on the Delaunay triangulation (which includes MST edges).

Our implementation treats categories as **mutually exclusive**:
```python
if (idx_i, idx_j) in mst_edges:
    category = 'mst'
elif (idx_i, idx_j) in delaunay_edges:
    category = 'delaunay'
elif (idx_i, idx_j) in neighbor_2_edges:
    category = 'neighbor_2'
else:
    category = 'neighbor_3'
```

And our quotas:
```python
quota_mst: 0.50
quota_delaunay: 0.20
quota_neighbor_2: 0.25
quota_neighbor_3: 0.05
```

These sum to 1.00, i.e., mutually exclusive. This means:
- We penalize "delaunay" edges after 20% of edges are Delaunay-only
- The paper allows 70% Delaunay (cumulative), meaning ~20% Delaunay-only is consistent
- **The mismatch is in neighbor quotas**: we allow 25% neighbor-2, but Table III shows only 18.3% are 2-neighbors in real grids

Actually, looking more carefully: Table VI's quotas might be cumulative (50% MST ⊂ 70% Delaunay) OR they might be the paper's internal targets. The paper says *"setting a quota of lines which may be added from each category"*. If MST and Delaunay are separate categories in their implementation (as in ours), the quotas should be mutually exclusive.

**This needs careful re-examination.** If the paper's quotas are cumulative and ours are exclusive, the effective constraint on neighbor edges is different, which changes how many long, mesh-forming edges get added.

**Test:** Compare the category breakdown of our generated lines against Table VI. If our MST fraction matches but Delaunay is lower, the quota interpretation may differ.

## Hypothesis 7: The 138 kV Problem Is a Scale Effect, Not a Parameter Problem

At 345 kV (259 nodes), there are roughly 1500 candidate edges. At 138 kV (3270 nodes), there are roughly 20,000+ candidates. But:
- Candidate pool grows as ~6n (Delaunay) + neighbors
- Target edges grow as 1.28n
- Available edges per target edge decreases as n grows

More importantly, at 138 kV:
- Mean line length is 13.1 km (vs 38.1 km at 345 kV) — much shorter
- Node density is ~13× higher
- The Delaunay triangulation produces much shorter edges on average
- The distance penalty w_dist=0.6/km penalizes a 13 km line by only -7.8, while connectivity bonus is +300

This means connectivity bonus dominates even more at 138 kV than at 345 kV. Every degree-0 node gets connected via whatever short edge is closest, creating stubs. The short-line bias is less of a problem (all lines are short), but the spur-creation problem is amplified.

Additionally, **DC flow is disabled** for 138 kV in our sweep for speed. Without DC flow guidance, there is no incentive for power-corridor-forming edges. The entire line selection is driven by distance + connectivity + quotas.

**Test:** Enable DC flow at 138 kV (accept the ~10× slowdown) and see if line length distribution and deg1_frac improve. The DC bonus should favor edges that carry flow, which tend to be longer corridor-forming edges.

## Hypothesis 8: The Paper's Algorithm Produces Tree-Like Topologies — And That's Correct

The paper targets m/n = 1.22. A tree has m/n = (n-1)/n ≈ 1.0. The paper adds only 22% more edges than a spanning tree. This is inherently a near-tree topology.

Meshedness at m/n = 1.22:
- independent_cycles = m - n + 1 = 0.22n
- max_cycles = 2n - 5
- meshedness ≈ 0.22n / (2n) ≈ 0.11

At m/n = 1.55 (Texas-7k 345 kV):
- independent_cycles = 0.55n
- meshedness ≈ 0.55 / 2 ≈ 0.275

We measured Texas-7k meshedness at 0.281, consistent with this formula. Our generated topology also gets 0.287. **Meshedness is almost entirely determined by m/n ratio, not by the algorithm's behavior.** It's a mathematical identity, not an independent metric.

Similarly, mean degree = 2 × m/n. At m/n = 1.55, mean_degree ≈ 3.10. This is also just arithmetic.

The metrics that actually carry information about topology SHAPE (independent of m/n) are:
- **Degree distribution shape** (not just mean)
- **Line length distribution** (not just mean)
- **Spatial clustering of edges** (are cycles local or spread out?)
- **Articulation point count**

The Birchfield paper does not validate against degree distribution shape. It validates against:
1. m/n ≈ 1.22 ✓
2. Category quotas (Table III/VI) ✓
3. Line intersection rate < 20% ✓
4. AC power flow convergence (not implemented)
5. N-1 connectivity ✓

**We may be asking the algorithm to match criteria it was never designed for.** The degree distribution mismatch might be acceptable by the paper's own standards.
